One-Way ANOVA

Move from dividing variance to testing hypotheses. Learn how the F-statistic compares experimental group signals against within-group noise to prove true effects.

ShopQuick Case Study (Continued)

Having decomposed our variance, we now perform a formal hypothesis test. The Product Team wants to prove that **at least one checkout layout is significantly faster than the others**.

The **One-Way ANOVA F-test** answers this by comparing the **between-design signal** (Treatment Mean Square) to the **user-to-user noise** (Error Mean Square). If the ratio ($F$) is sufficiently large, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the layout designs indeed perform differently.

The ANOVA Linear Model:
$$Y_{ij}$$ = $$\mu$$ + $$\tau_j$$ + $$\varepsilon_{ij}$$
$Y_{ij}$ A user's checkout time (in seconds).
$\mu$ Overall average checkout time across all users.
$\tau_j$ The effect of Checkout Layout $j$ ($\bar{y}_j - \mu$). A faster layout yields a negative effect!
$\varepsilon_{ij}$ Individual user noise (checkout speed, connection lag) around their design average.

Running multiple t-tests inflates the **Family-wise Error Rate (FWER)**. The probability of committing at least one Type I error (false positive) across $m$ independent tests is: $$\alpha_{\text{FW}} = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$$ If you compare 5 groups, you have $m = \frac{5(4)}{2} = 10$ pairwise comparisons. At $\alpha=0.05$, your compound risk of a false positive rises to $1 - (0.95)^{10} \approx 40.1\%$.

The One-Way ANOVA F-test resolves this by running a **single, joint omnibus test** that compares the variance between groups against the variance within groups. It tests the global null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k$ at a constant, controlled $\alpha$ level (typically 5%).

ANOVA has three primary assumptions:

  1. Independence of Observations: Observations must be independent both within and between groups. This is the most critical assumption; violations cannot be corrected and invalidate the test.
  2. Normality: The residuals must be normally distributed within each group ($\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$). ANOVA is highly robust to violations of normality when sample sizes are moderate to large ($N \ge 15$ per group) due to the Central Limit Theorem.
  3. Homoscedasticity: The variance of residuals must be equal across all groups. ANOVA is robust to unequal variances **only if** group sample sizes are equal. If sample sizes are unequal and variances differ significantly, the Type I error rate becomes heavily distorted (Welch's ANOVA must be used instead).

For a comparison between exactly two groups ($k = 2$), the F-statistic from a One-Way ANOVA is mathematically equivalent to the square of the t-statistic from a two-sample pooled t-test: $$F = t^2$$ Consequently, the p-value yielded by both tests will be identical. The F-test in this setting is simply a two-tailed version of the t-test.

A significant F-test only rejects the null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k$, meaning **at least one group mean is significantly different** from another. It does not identify *which* specific groups differ, nor how many differences exist.

To locate the differences, you must perform **post-hoc pairwise comparisons** (such as Tukey's HSD, Scheffé's test, or Bonferroni adjustments) which are designed to control the Family-wise Error Rate while conducting multiple comparisons.
